The Decay Ribbon

A satellite's death is not a number on a spreadsheet. It is a physical event — a slow, luminous unraveling traced against the black. Scroll, and watch the ribbon fall.

01 Stable orbit

For eight years, the line held.

At four hundred kilometres, KESTREL-9 moved at a steady seven-point-six-six kilometres per second — fast enough that the Earth's gravity only ever curved its fall into a circle. The telemetry ribbon was calm: a thin cyan thread, drifting with the gentle sine of a healthy orbit.

Nothing about the descent had begun. The ribbon simply existed, patient, in the cold.

Orbital decay is not a failure. It is a countdown that started the day the rocket stopped firing.

03 Drag

Then the air remembered.

Even at four hundred kilometres, the atmosphere is not empty. A handful of stray molecules grazed the hull every orbit, stealing a whisper of velocity. Each graze lowered the perigee by metres. The ribbon's altitude began to drop, and with it, its calm.

The eccentricity climbed. What was a smooth thread started to wobble — the first sign that the orbit was no longer a circle but a slowing, sagging ellipse.

04 Instability

The ribbon began to shake.

Below three hundred kilometres the drag compounds. Every pass through the densest part of the orbit bleeds more speed, deepens the dip, thickens the wobble. The telemetry no longer reads as a line so much as a tremor — the satellite fighting to hold its shape against a sky that is closing in.

05 Reentry interface

At one hundred and twenty kilometres, physics turns hostile.

This is the reentry interface — the boundary where the thin vacuum of space becomes a wall of gas. The satellite is no longer falling through emptiness. It is plowing through matter at seven kilometres a second, compressing the air in front of it until that air turns to plasma.

The ribbon's colour is about to change.

Atmospheric friction is the only thing that ever truly stops a satellite. And it does not stop gently.

07 Ablation

The ribbon catches fire.

Below the interface the cyan thread ignites. Plasma sheathes the hull; the stroke burns to magenta. Sparks peel away from the line as components vaporise — solar arrays first, then the bus, then the instruments that spent eight years watching the world. The visible length of the ribbon shortens as it is consumed, segment by segment.

08 Terminal phase

Thirty seconds. Then nothing.

The countdown is not dramatic in the way films promise. There is no explosion, only a brightening and a fading. The ribbon narrows to a point, the sparks thin to a scatter, and the telemetry flatlines. Where a machine had been, there is only a faint trail of ionised air, cooling in the dark.

T-00:00 · ORBIT TERMINATED

KESTREL-9 has completed its final descent. The ribbon is gone. Somewhere in the upper atmosphere, a few grams of metal are still glowing, and falling, and forgetting they were ever anything else.
