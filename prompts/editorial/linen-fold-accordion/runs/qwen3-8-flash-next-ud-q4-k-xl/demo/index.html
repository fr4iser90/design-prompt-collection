On the Fold

A napkin is never merely a napkin. Pressed, creased, and squared, it becomes a small act of order — the first gesture a diner meets before a single plate arrives. This issue treats the page the same way: each chapter folded beneath the last, waiting to be opened.

Below, unfold the sections in any order. The creases are the seams; pull them open and the cloth gives way to technique, produce, and the discipline that binds them.

Fig. 01 — ReductionSteel · Steam

A reduction is not a shortcut. It is the slow, deliberate removal of water until only the essence remains.

Begin with a stock that has already earned its depth — bones roasted, aromatics sweated, time given its due. The reduction concentrates what is already true; it cannot invent flavour from nothing. Watch the nappe: the moment a spoon leaves a clean line across the back of the bowl, the sauce is finished.

Control the heat so that the surface trembles rather than boils. A rolling boil dulls delicate aromatics and clouds the finish. Precision here is patience made visible.

Chef's Note
Reduce until the spoon hesitates. That hesitation is the dish telling you it is ready.
— Marguerite Aubry, Chef de Cuisine
  • RatioReduce to one-third of the starting volume
  • HeatGentle simmer, never a hard boil
  • TestNappe — the line holds on the spoon
Fig. 02 — The CutHands · Steel

The knife is an extension of intent. A clean cut damages less, cooks evenly, and lets the ingredient speak without bruising.

Grip the handle firmly and let the knuckles of the guiding hand form a cage around the blade. The edge should meet the board, not the fingers. Speed follows accuracy, never the reverse — a fast, careless cut is only a slow one that has not yet been corrected.

Keep the edge honed daily and sharpened weekly. A dull knife crushes cells, releases moisture, and dulls the colour of everything it touches.

Chef's Note
Respect the blade and it will never betray you. Neglect it once and it will.
— Tomás Reyes, Sous Chef
  • GripPinch grip — thumb and forefinger on the blade
  • GuardClaw hand, knuckles forward
  • EdgeHone each shift, sharpen weekly
Fig. 03 — The MarketAutumn · Harvest

The menu is written by the season, not by the chef. Our only task is to listen and respond.

Early autumn brings the last of the stone fruit and the first of the squash, the apples at their most taut and sweet. Build the plate around what is at its peak and let restraint do the work — a single perfect element needs no decoration.

Buy close, buy often, buy little. Freshness is a perishable ingredient in its own right, and it cannot be bought in advance.

Chef's Note
When the tomato is perfect, put down the salt and simply get out of the way.
— Marguerite Aubry, Chef de Cuisine
  • NowFig, quince, chanterelle, blackberry
  • ApproachingChestnut, endive, late pear
  • RuleOne plate, one peak ingredient
Fig. 04 — The PassService · Order

Service is choreography. Every plate crosses the pass on cue, or the whole performance falters.

Call the order, confirm the count, wipe the rim. The pass is where cooking becomes hospitality — the last moment before intention reaches the guest. Composure under pressure is not the absence of stress but its organisation.

Communicate in complete sentences. Ambiguity in a hot kitchen becomes a cold plate, and a cold plate is a broken promise.

Chef's Note
A kitchen is calm or it is chaos. There is no middle, and the guest always feels the difference.
— Tomás Reyes, Sous Chef
  • CallState the count, confirm the count
  • WipeClean the rim before it leaves
  • VoiceSpeak in full, clear sentences
