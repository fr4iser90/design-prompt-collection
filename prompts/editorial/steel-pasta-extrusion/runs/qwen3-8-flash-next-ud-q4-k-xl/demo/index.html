Extruded Narrative

A long-form study of pasta as a manufacturing problem — where every strand you read is being pulled, thinned, and stamped into shape. The medium is the machine. Scroll to feel the die.

01Feed

Every extrusion begins as a refusal to flow.

Durum semolina is not a flour. It is a coarse, amber grit — the heart of the wheat kernel, milled hard enough to resist water. Hydrated to thirty-one percent, it becomes a paste that wants to hold its shape against every force you apply.

In the feed chamber, the screw does two jobs at once: it conveys the mass forward and it compresses it. Volume collapses. Air is driven out through a vacuum port. What leaves the throat of the barrel is no longer loose grain — it is a dense, homogeneous slug, ready to be forced through a hole it cannot naturally fit.

“The die does not create the shape. It reveals the shape the pressure was always trying to make.”
— Process note, line 04

02Compression

Under load, starch and protein negotiate a truce.

The barrel is a pressure vessel. As the screw advances, resistance climbs toward 140 bar. In that squeeze, the two proteins of durum — glutenin and gliadin — uncoil and knit into a continuous network: the gluten matrix. Starch granules are packed into its mesh like aggregate in concrete.

Temperature is the third variable. Too cold and the dough fractures; too hot and the proteins denature before they can bind. The extruder holds the mass at a precise 38 °C, the narrow band where the material behaves like a plastic solid rather than a liquid or a crumb.

“Reading this page is extrusion. Your attention is the pressure; the text is the dough.”
— On the medium

03The Die

A brass plate, perforated, decides everything.

The die is the soul of the machine. A single disc of brass — chosen because it grips the dough just enough to polish the surface as it passes. Its perforations are machined to tolerances measured in hundredths of a millimetre.

For spaghetti, the holes are round. For rigatoni, they are rectangular and paired with a central pin. For penne, the pin sits at an angle, so the strand shears into a tube with a bevelled mouth. The die is a grammar: one plate, one sentence of geometry, repeated forever.

“Tension is not a flaw in the line. Tension is what keeps the line from snapping.”
— On the strands below

04Extrusion

The moment the impossible shape becomes a line.

The slug meets the die and has nowhere else to go. It is forced through apertures narrower than the dough itself — and emerges, impossibly, as a continuous strand. This is the paradox at the heart of extrusion: matter that cannot fit, made to fit, without tearing.

Downstream, the strands are stretched by a pulling belt. The tension aligns the gluten, straightens the tube, and thins the wall. Read down this column and you feel it — the letter-spacing opening, the line-height pulling taut, the whole page vibrating like a machine under load.

“Cut, dry, and the shape is frozen in time. The narrative hardens into product.”
— Final stage

05Setting

Dryness is the last act of the die.

Fresh strands are limp and sticky. They enter a drying tunnel at descending temperature and rising airflow, losing water slowly so the surface does not crack. As moisture falls below twelve percent, the gluten network sets — the geometry the die imposed becomes permanent.

Then the cutter falls. A rotating blade, timed to the belt, slices the endless line into measured lengths. Spaghetti becomes spaghetti. The story stops being a process and starts being a thing — engineered, precise, ready to be boiled back into softness by someone else’s heat.
